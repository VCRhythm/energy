Chaos & Fractals

Deterministic, yet unpredictable

Chaotic systems follow exact rules but are so sensitive to their starting point that long-term prediction becomes impossible — the "butterfly effect". Their geometry is often fractal: detailed at every scale. Three icons of the field below.

The Logistic Map

A toy population model: xₙ₊₁ = r · xₙ · (1 − xₙ). As you raise the growth rate r, the system period-doubles — 1 value, then 2, 4, 8 … — and tips into chaos. The cobweb plot shows the iteration; the bifurcation diagram shows every long-term value for every r at once.

Regime:
Try this: sweep r slowly from 2.9 to 4.0. Watch the cobweb settle to one point, split to a 2-cycle near r≈3.0, cascade, then scatter. The pink line marks your r on the bifurcation map — find the period-3 window near r≈3.83.

The Lorenz Attractor

Edward Lorenz's simplified model of atmospheric convection. The trajectory never repeats and never escapes — it loops forever around two wings, a strange attractor. This is the system that gave us the butterfly effect.

Default ρ = 28 is the famous chaotic regime. Drop ρ below ~24 and the butterfly collapses to a stable point; push it higher for wilder orbits.

The Mandelbrot Set

For each point c in the plane, iterate z → z² + c from zero. Points that stay bounded are in the set (dark); the colors outside count how fast they escape. The boundary is infinitely intricate — a true fractal.

Zoom: 1.0×
Try this: left-click to zoom in, right-click to zoom out. Dive into the boundary and you'll find smaller copies of the whole set — the hallmark of self-similarity.
