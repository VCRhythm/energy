Networks & Power Laws

Why a few hubs rule the network

The web, social ties, protein interactions, airline routes — real networks look nothing like random graphs. A handful of nodes hoard most of the connections, and any two nodes are just a few hops apart. Two models explain why.

Preferential Attachment (Barabási–Albert)

Grow a network one node at a time. Each newcomer links to existing nodes with probability proportional to how many links they already have — the rich get richer. The result is "scale-free": a few enormous hubs, a long tail of small nodes, and a power-law degree distribution (a straight line on log-log axes).

Small-World (Watts–Strogatz)

Start from an orderly ring where everyone knows their neighbors. Rewire just a few edges into random long-range shortcuts and something remarkable happens: the network stays highly clustered (your friends know each other) yet the distance between any two nodes plummets — "six degrees of separation".
